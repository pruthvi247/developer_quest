## What is happening

The innovation initiative has generated substantial activity: more than 12,000 ideas in three years. Yet funding is concentrated at headquarters and large regional offices, store-originated ideas remain underrepresented, and several funded initiatives struggle during execution. Together, these outcomes indicate a three-stage imbalance rather than a single participation issue.

| **Stage** | **Observed or inferred failure** | **Business consequence** |
|----|----|----|
| 1\. Discovery | Frontline employees face time, access, confidence, language, and format barriers. Practical workarounds may never be recognized as scalable ideas. | RetailCo misses frequent, local, customer-facing problems. |
| 2\. Evaluation | Polished proposals and HQ-aligned language may receive an advantage; frontline feasibility is tested too late. | Proposal quality becomes a proxy for insight quality. |
| 3\. Execution | Funding may precede pilots, clear ownership, and stakeholder alignment. | Operational realities and resistance surface after commitment. |

## Why it matters

The risk is larger than unequal representation. RetailCo could allocate capital to ideas that present well but fail in stores, while overlooking repeatable improvements visible only to employees closest to customers. This weakens customer relevance, slows innovation, wastes implementation effort, and teaches frontline employees that participation does not change outcomes.

## What the CEO should do

- Reframe the program from idea collection to customer-problem discovery. Build a problem repository that groups recurring pain points by customer, journey, location, and store type.

- Remove access and confidence friction. Allow voice, video, photo, and three-question submissions in local languages, supported by protected time and innovation ambassadors.

- Separate insight quality from business-case writing. Use blind first-pass screening where practical, mentoring, and diverse panels with real frontline decision rights.

- Route ideas into three lanes: Fix It Now for local reversible changes; Test and Learn for evidence-building pilots; Reinvent the Experience for a small strategic portfolio.

- Fund evidence in stages. Pilot in 5 to 10 representative stores before scale, with customer, operational, financial, and adoption measures defined in advance.

- Create execution accountability. Every funded idea needs an executive sponsor, business owner, and frontline champion, with 30/60/90-day reviews.

- Measure the full funnel and impact, not just volume: access, conversion, feedback, pilot success, implementation, adoption, customer outcomes, and business value.

## Expected outcome

The redesign creates a reinforcing flywheel: more store insights lead to more customer-relevant experiments; better experiments raise pilot success and implementation confidence; visible outcomes increase employee belief and repeat participation; and that participation produces better insights. The goal is not to fund store ideas because they come from stores. The goal is to ensure every valuable insight has a fair path to evidence and impact.

# 1. Context, Facts and Assumptions

## Case facts

- Horizon RetailCo operates more than 2,000 stores across metropolitan, urban, and emerging markets.

- More than 12,000 ideas were submitted over three years.

- Nearly 70% of funded ideas came from headquarters and large regional offices, which represent less than 35% of the workforce.

- Less than 15% of funded ideas came from store employees, who represent the majority of the workforce and interact with customers daily.

- Several funded ideas faced implementation challenges linked to operational realities, local customer needs, or stakeholder resistance.

# 2. Problem Statement and Core Diagnosis

RetailCo’s real challenge is not whether headquarters or store employees generate better ideas. It is whether the organization has designed a reliable mechanism to discover important customer and operational problems, convert rough observations into testable concepts, select ideas using relevant evidence, and scale solutions that work across varied store environments.

## The three-stage imbalance

| **Idea generation** | **Idea evaluation** | **Idea implementation** |
|----|----|----|
| Opportunity gap: shift schedules, submission friction, language, confidence, and limited coaching may suppress frontline insights. | Translation gap: evaluators may reward financial articulation, completeness, and alignment with central priorities. | Validation gap: store managers, operations, supply chain, technology, and customers may enter after funding rather than before it. |
| Result: fewer store observations enter the funnel. | Result: insight quality may be confused with proposal quality. | Result: feasibility and resistance are discovered after budget is committed. |

## First-principles test

Instead of asking, “How do we get more ideas from stores?”, ask: “What must be true for RetailCo to consistently discover, evaluate, and implement valuable ideas?”

- Employees closest to a problem must be able to capture it with minimal friction.

- A rough but important insight must not lose to a polished but weak proposal.

- Evaluation must include customer value, strategic fit, operational feasibility, and local relevance.

- Reversible ideas must be tested cheaply before large commitments.

- Every approved idea must have ownership, resources, decision rights, and success measures.

- Every participant must receive feedback so the system learns and trust compounds.

# 3. Root Cause Analysis

## People and culture

- Store employees may not identify everyday workarounds as innovation. A local “jugaad” can be a scalable process improvement, but the system may never invite employees to frame it that way.

- Confidence and status differences can reduce participation, particularly when successful submissions appear to require corporate language, financial models, or presentations.

- Managers may support innovation rhetorically but prioritize immediate service levels, sales, and staffing during shifts.

- If employees rarely see outcomes or receive feedback, participation becomes a low-reward behavior and repeat engagement declines.

## Process and access

- A long, essay-style submission creates unnecessary friction for shop-floor contributors.

- An open-ended “submit any idea” invitation gives insufficient direction and produces uneven relevance.

- The process may collect isolated solutions before establishing which customer or operational problems recur across stores.

- Small reversible changes and high-risk strategic concepts may compete in one queue despite requiring different evidence and governance.

## Evaluation and governance

- Panel composition may limit frontline and local-market context.

- Standard scoring may overvalue proposal completeness and undervalue customer proximity or frequency of the problem.

- Blind screening can reduce some identity and location signals, but cannot replace diverse judgment and evidence.

- Funding approval may be treated as the finish line, while implementation ownership remains unclear.

## Experimentation and execution

- Ideas may move from presentation to funding without representative-store pilots.

- Operational stakeholders may be consulted after design, making resistance predictable rather than surprising.

- The organization may treat most ideas as irreversible commitments, causing either excessive caution or oversized bets.

- Metrics may emphasize submissions and approvals rather than adoption, customer outcomes, business impact, and learning.

## Habit loop for organizational change

| **Cue** | **Routine** | **Reward** |
|----|----|----|
| A monthly customer mission, recurring problem review, or shift huddle prompt. | Each store records one customer frustration, one operational frustration, and one improvement or experiment. | Specific feedback, visible implementation, recognition of learning, and local authority to act. |
| Purpose: make problem discovery expected and timely. | Purpose: make innovation part of work rather than an annual competition. | Purpose: reinforce the belief that “people like me can influence the company.” |

## Lever 1: Discover the right problems and remove access friction

Objective: increase the quality and diversity of customer and operational insights entering the funnel, without forcing frontline employees to become business-case writers.

- Launch a Frontline Insights Program. Quarterly innovation briefs should name customer missions, such as reducing waiting for working parents or helping elderly customers navigate the store.

- Create a problem repository, not just an idea database. Tag recurring issues by journey, customer segment, store type, region, frequency, severity, and current workaround.

- Offer shop-floor submission in under five minutes through voice note, short video, photograph, customer quote with personal details removed, or three structured questions: What did you observe? Why does it matter? What would better look like?

- Provide local-language support, manager-assisted submission, and protected huddle time.

- Appoint one innovation ambassador per approximately 20 stores, subject to workload validation. Ambassadors harvest, translate, combine, and champion insights.

- Create a Store Wisdom Library of two-minute practices, before-and-after experiments, customer tips, local merchandising lessons, and documented failures.

## Lever 2: Evaluate insight before polish

Objective: preserve merit while preventing corporate writing skill, location, or sponsorship from becoming accidental selection criteria.

- Use a two-stage review: frontline validation first, then strategic assessment.

- Apply blind first-pass screening where identity and location are not essential to context.

- Use idea mentors to convert rough observations into hypotheses, evidence plans, and simple business cases.

- Include store employees, a store manager, operations, customer experience, and relevant functional experts. Give frontline representatives decision rights, including the ability to pause progression when feasibility evidence is missing.

- Score on problem importance, customer value, evidence, feasibility, scalability, strategic fit, and learning potential. Use different scorecards for different lanes.

- Return feedback to every submitter: review status, decision rationale, required evidence, duplicate linkages, and whether the problem is already being addressed.

## Lever 3: Create three investment lanes

| **Lane** | **Use case** | **Funding and governance** | **Success test** |
|----|----|----|----|
| 1\. Fix It Now | Signage, placement, queue communication, or simple workflow changes that are local and reversible. | Store manager authority within guardrails; minimal paperwork. | Fast adoption, no safety or compliance harm, measurable local improvement. |
| 2\. Test and Learn | Service formats, assortment changes, staffing patterns, digital assistance, returns, or checkout changes. | Small pilot budget; 5 to 10 representative stores; predefined measures. | Customer value and operational feasibility demonstrated before scale. |
| 3\. Reinvent the Experience | New store journey, omnichannel service, loyalty experience, business model, or differentiated proposition. | Small strategic portfolio; executive sponsor; staged funding; cross-functional team. | Distinctive customer value, strategic differentiation, viable economics, and scalable operating model. |

This architecture incorporates both continuous improvement and breakthrough innovation without forcing them to compete on identical criteria. A reserved portion of funding can support high-risk, contrarian, and experimental concepts, but the exact percentage should be set only after RetailCo defines risk appetite and portfolio capacity.

## Lever 4: Pilot before scale and treat reversible decisions differently

- Classify decisions as reversible or hard to reverse. Most local pilots are reversible and should move quickly within guardrails.

- Run pilots in a representative set of stores rather than only flagship locations. Include metropolitan, urban, and emerging-market formats when relevant.

- Define baseline, target, duration, owner, customer measure, operational measure, financial measure, and stop/scale criteria before launch.

- Document failed prototypes and the learning they produced. A well-designed failed experiment is valuable when it prevents larger waste or changes the hypothesis.

- Build scale playbooks only after success: process, technology, training, staffing, economics, dependencies, and local adaptation rules.

## Lever 5: Make ownership and innovation habits explicit

- Assign every funded idea an executive sponsor, accountable business owner, and frontline champion.

- Track progress at 30, 60, and 90 days, with exceptions escalated to a portfolio owner.

- Create category or experience champions for domains such as checkout, returns, elderly customers, sustainability, digital assistance, and festival merchandising. Each champion owns one outcome and proposes monthly improvements.

- Add measured innovation enablement to manager reviews, including team participation quality, feedback closure, experiment support, and implemented outcomes. Avoid rewarding raw submission volume.

- Recognize builders, pilot teams, shared practices, customer insights, and strong learning from failed experiments, not only the original idea generator.

- Publish store-originated success stories and show how small, rough observations became scalable solutions.

# 8. Risks and Mitigations

| **Risk** | **Why it could happen** | **Mitigation** |
|----|----|----|
| Participation theater | Targets drive superficial submissions. | Measure recurring problems, evidence quality, repeat participation, and outcomes rather than raw volume. |
| Reverse bias or tokenism | Store origin becomes a positive quota rather than a source of context. | Use transparent lane-specific criteria and blind review where feasible. |
| Operational overload | Managers and staff lack time for innovation duties. | Protected time, bounded pilots, ambassador coverage, and explicit capacity limits. |
| Local fragmentation | Too much autonomy creates inconsistent customer experience. | Enterprise guardrails, reversible local authority, and scale standards. |
| Pilot purgatory | Many tests run but few decisions are made. | Fixed decision dates, stop/scale criteria, and accountable portfolio owners. |
| Veto gridlock | Frontline pause rights become an indefinite block. | Require evidence-based concerns, time-boxed resolution, and escalation paths. |
| Weak economics | Enthusiasm outruns value. | Stage funding and increase financial rigor as evidence matures. |
| Technology or privacy risk | Voice, video, and customer quotes capture sensitive data. | Simple privacy rules, de-identification, access controls, and approved platforms. |
| Recognition inequity | Sponsors or presenters receive credit instead of builders. | Track originators, mentors, pilot teams, and implementers separately. |
| Copyable innovation | Improvements help but do not differentiate. | Maintain a breakthrough lane and ask whether the experience creates distinctive capabilities or data advantages. |

# 9. Panel Discussion Script

## Opening: 60 to 90 seconds

“The headline is encouraging and concerning at the same time. RetailCo has no shortage of ideas: employees submitted more than 12,000 in three years. But the outcomes show that the system is not reliably converting the company’s broadest customer knowledge into implemented value. Nearly 70% of funded ideas come from locations representing less than 35% of the workforce, store-originated ideas remain below 15%, and several funded proposals fail when they meet operational reality. I would not conclude that store ideas are automatically better, or that the panel is deliberately biased. I would conclude that RetailCo must inspect the full funnel. My diagnosis is a three-stage imbalance in discovery, evaluation, and execution. My recommendation is to redesign innovation as a customer-problem-solving operating system, with easier frontline capture, fairer evaluation, small representative pilots, and clear implementation ownership.”

## Explain the diagnosis: 2 minutes

“At the discovery stage, store employees may face an opportunity gap: less uninterrupted time, a format that favors essays, and lower confidence that everyday workarounds count as innovation. At evaluation, a polished business case may be mistaken for a superior insight, particularly if panels lack store context. At execution, stakeholders appear to be involved after the funding decision, so feasibility and resistance are discovered too late. These are hypotheses, not facts. My first step would be to validate them through funnel data, panel analysis, and observation in representative stores.”

## Present the solution: 3 to 4 minutes

- “First, shift from generic idea collection to focused customer-problem discovery. Publish quarterly customer missions and create a repository of recurring problems.”

- “Second, lower the friction. Let an employee submit a voice note, photo, short video, or three answers in under five minutes. Mentors can build the business case later.”

- “Third, preserve merit through a two-stage review: frontline validation followed by strategic assessment. Use blind first-pass screening where context allows and give frontline representatives real decision rights.”

- “Fourth, create three lanes: fix reversible local issues immediately; pilot uncertain ideas in 5 to 10 representative stores; reserve a small strategic portfolio for reinvention.”

- “Fifth, make funding the start of accountability, not the end of selection. Every funded idea gets an executive sponsor, accountable business owner, frontline champion, and 30/60/90-day measures.”

## Close: 30 seconds

“The objective is not to make the portfolio look more inclusive by funding weaker ideas. It is to stop losing strong customer insights because they arrived in the wrong format, from the wrong location, or without an executive translator. RetailCo should reward neither headquarters nor stores. It should reward verified customer value and implementation. When the process makes frontline insight easy to capture, evidence easy to generate, and ownership impossible to avoid, the strongest ideas have a better chance to rise and succeed.”

# 10. Anticipated Panel Questions and Model Answers

Q: Are you assuming bias without evidence?

> A: No. The funding concentration is a signal, not proof. I would test the submission mix, conversion by stage, evaluator scoring, proposal quality, and pilot outcomes. My recommendations improve decision quality even if explicit bias is not found.

Q: Why should store ideas receive special treatment?

> A: They should not receive a lower merit bar. They need an equal path to articulation and evidence because store employees have customer proximity but may lack time and business-case skills. The unit of evaluation should be the problem and evidence, not the submitter’s status.

Q: What if headquarters simply produces better ideas?

> A: That is possible. The correct response is to compare quality-adjusted conversion and outcomes. If HQ ideas outperform after equivalent mentoring and pilot design, fund them. But RetailCo should first ensure the funnel is not filtering out valuable observations before they can be developed.

Q: Would blind screening remove necessary context?

> A: It should be used only in the first pass and only for identity signals that are not essential. Store type, customer segment, and operational context may be critical. Blind review is one control, not the entire evaluation model.

Q: Is one ambassador per 20 stores realistic?

> A: It is a starting design hypothesis, not a fixed ratio. Pilot different coverage models and measure workload, submission quality, feedback closure, and implementation support before scaling.

Q: Why fund operational ideas?

> A: Operational ideas can improve customer experience, cost, productivity, safety, availability, and consistency. They should be funded when the problem is material, the change needs cross-store evidence or resources, and expected value exceeds the cost of learning and implementation.

Q: How do you prevent thousands of low-quality submissions?

> A: Use focused problem briefs, structured capture, duplicate clustering, ambassadors, and a local Fix It Now lane. The system should harvest observations and recurring problems, not push every thought into an executive queue.

Q: How do you balance speed with control?

> A: Match governance to reversibility. Small local changes move quickly within guardrails. Cross-functional or irreversible decisions require stronger evidence and sponsorship. This avoids treating every idea as equally risky.

Q: How would you select pilot stores?

> A: Select stores that represent the conditions needed to disprove the idea: different formats, regions, demand patterns, staffing levels, and customer segments. Do not test only where success is easiest.

Q: What does success look like after six months?

> A: Higher eligible and repeat participation from underrepresented groups, faster feedback, a visible set of recurring problems, more representative pilots, explicit stop/scale decisions, and early customer or operational impact. Funding shares alone are insufficient.

Q: Should managers be evaluated on participation?

> A: Partly, but not on volume. Evaluate whether they create access, close feedback, support experiments, and sustain implemented improvements. A raw quota could encourage low-quality submissions.

Q: What is the most important recommendation?

> A: The pilot-first, full-funnel redesign. Access without fair evaluation creates frustration; fair evaluation without experimentation creates debate; pilots without ownership create shelfware. The mechanisms work as one system.

Q: How is this future-oriented?

> A: It separates continuous improvement from breakthrough innovation, reserves capacity for contrarian experiments, develops frontline problem-solving capability, and turns local learning into reusable enterprise knowledge.

Q: What would you stop doing?

> A: I would stop celebrating submission volume as the primary success measure, stop requiring complete business cases at entry, stop funding without representative feasibility evidence, and stop treating rejection without feedback as acceptable.

Q: Where does the Decathlon analogy help?

> A: The useful principle is not to copy a retailer wholesale. It is to empower local experts to contribute relevant learning and take initiative. Decathlon India’s careers material emphasizes trust and freedom to take initiative, while a published customer story describes employee-generated, localized learning at scale. RetailCo can adapt that principle through category champions and a Store Wisdom Library.

Q: How would you respond if the CEO has no additional budget?

> A: Reallocate rather than add: move a portion of large-project funding into small pilot tranches, use existing shift huddles, rotate ambassador duties, simplify the current platform, and stop low-value reporting. Prove the mechanism before requesting scale investment.

# 11. Experience-Led Presentation Narrative

Use one employee and one customer journey to make the system tangible. Keep the character illustrative and explicitly label it as a composite, not a case fact.

## Opening scene

“Imagine a store associate who watches customers abandon a queue at 7:00 p.m. every Friday. She has already developed a simple workaround with her manager, but she does not call it innovation. The company’s form asks for ROI, scalability, and an implementation plan, so the observation never enters the system. Meanwhile, a well-presented central proposal receives funding, reaches stores, and creates extra work at peak time. Neither outcome proves that stores are smarter or headquarters is wrong. It proves the process is selecting for readiness before it has validated reality.”

## Transformation scene

“In the redesigned system, Friday’s customer mission is visible in the shift huddle. The associate records a 45-second voice note. Similar observations from 27 stores are grouped in the problem repository. A mentor develops two hypotheses. A diverse panel routes one change to Fix It Now and another to a 10-store pilot. The pilot shows lower abandonment without adding labor. The business owner converts the result into a scale playbook, and the associate and pilot team are recognized as builders. The next month, more employees contribute because they can see how insight becomes impact.”

## Why this story works

- It humanizes the opportunity gap without claiming store ideas are inherently superior.

- It demonstrates the mechanism from observation to scale.

- It links inclusivity to customer value and capital discipline.

- It gives the panel memorable anchors: voice note, recurring problem, representative pilot, owner, and flywheel.

- Is this a real and important human problem?

- Is the proposed experience meaningfully better and simpler for the customer?

- What evidence would change our mind?

- Can we test it cheaply and reversibly?

- Who owns implementation if the test succeeds?

# 13. Storyline Improvements and Argument Strengthening

## What is strongest in your current thinking

- The “opportunity gap, not ability gap” framing is inclusive without assuming weaker standards.

- The three-stage imbalance creates a logical bridge from symptoms to system redesign.

- The line “do not confuse business-case ability with insight value” is distinctive and defensible.

- The three-lane model resolves the tension between quick operational improvements and breakthrough innovation.

- The emphasis on feedback, pilots, and ownership closes the loop that many innovation proposals leave open.

## What to tighten

| **Current point** | **Risk** | **Stronger version** |
|----|----|----|
| “Evaluation should be data-driven.” | Early ideas rarely have complete data; this can recreate HQ advantage. | Say “evidence-led and stage-appropriate.” Use observations early, pilot data later, and economics before scale. |
| “Every store submits three items.” | Can sound like a quota and produce noise. | Present it as a monthly discovery ritual, sampled or phased, with quality and recurrence checks. |
| “Blind screening.” | Can remove relevant store and customer context. | Blind identity and sponsorship signals where possible, while preserving operational context. |
| “Store representative can veto.” | May create gridlock or token responsibility. | Give a time-bounded, evidence-based pause right with escalation. |
| “Reserve funding for high-risk ideas.” | No basis for a percentage. | Recommend a protected portfolio envelope to be set after risk appetite and capacity are defined. |
| Decathlon analogy | Some claims may be anecdotal or overgeneralized. | Use verified principles: initiative, local expertise, and employee-generated learning. Say RetailCo must adapt, not copy. |
| Book and leader references | Too many named lenses can make the case feel derivative. | Use one or two quietly in support. Lead with your own diagnosis and mechanisms. |
| BMW versus Maruti representatives | The intended analogy is unclear and may distract. | Either define the customer-expertise point precisely or omit it from the panel narrative. |

## Recommended storyline order

1.  Start with the paradox: strong participation, weak conversion to broad and implementable value.

2.  Resolve the executive debate by reframing it as a system question.

3.  Show the three-stage imbalance and distinguish fact from hypothesis.

4.  Introduce the operating system as the inevitable response to each root cause.

5.  Demonstrate the mechanism through one employee-customer story.

6.  End on impact, accountability, and the innovation flywheel.

# 14. Closing Note to the CEO

RetailCo’s advantage is not the number of ideas it can collect. It is the number of important customer problems it can see earlier than competitors, test more intelligently, and solve at scale.

The company should not choose between inclusion and merit. It should build a process in which wider participation improves discovery, stage-appropriate evidence protects merit, and clear ownership converts learning into value. When innovation becomes an everyday habit rather than an occasional competition, frontline employees stop being sources of suggestions and become owners of better customer outcomes.
